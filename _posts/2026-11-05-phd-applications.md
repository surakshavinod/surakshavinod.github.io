It is that time of the year when the due date for grad school applications in the U.S. is just around the corner; and I'm sure everyone applying is probably stress-reading their SOPs over and over again while also following up with their recommenders and double-checking every item on that submission checklist! I was also here, just almost two years ago -- what seems like forever ago now -- so trust me when I say, I know the boat you're in extremely well. That is precisely why I decided to write this post on things I wish I knew when I was applying to grad school. I truly hope that you find this useful.

### 1. Fit over prestige
Yes, that prestigious university is going to help you in the long-run, but you know what would help you overall? The right lab. PhD is mainly about the research that you do, so when you are shortlisting your universities, ensure you have at least 3 labs in the school that you are interested in and that align with your previous research background. You may pivot to a completely different research area when you actually start your PhD, but it is important that your experience aligns with at least some labs in the school during the application and interview phases. But this also doesn't mean you apply to 20-something schools, hoping you get into at least one school. Tailoring your application takes a lot of time and it inevitably shows when you have not done so. Put in the effort to make your application stand out to the few schools that you are actually interested in and can see yourself joining. Consider the area the school is located in as well. Will you happy there for at least 5 years? 

### 2. SOP should not be your CV in paragraphs
Your SOP should tell me what I cannot get from your CV. Weave a story through your research and life experiences that culminates in explaining why you want to pursue a PhD at this timepoint in that school. Include all the how's, why's, and what's. Try to connect each paragraph to the next. 

### 3. Your LORs should be from people that can speak to your skills
PhD is heavily research-based, therefore it is best to get your recommendation letters from individuals that can speak to those research skills and experience. For example, the professor you worked under for your undergraduate/master's thesis, internship advisor, work manager, etc. I would avoid asking teachers who've only thought you for a semester or two and with whom you have not worked with closely. The longer their letter, the better but as always, quality of the letter over quantity of the pages. Ensure your recommenders have a favorable opinion of you.

### 4. It is okay to not have (too many) publications
Having a publication, preprint, or a conference proceeding will definitely be add a lot of value to your application packet, but having none won't also take taking away any value or reflect negatively on you. A lot of students apply to PhD programs directly after their Bachelor's degree which means that there is a good percentage of applicants that do not have a publication yet.

### 5. It is helpful to contact the professors beforehand, but it is okay if you were unable to do so 
While it is helpful if you can contact the professors beforehand, it is okay if you are not able to. If you are contacting the professors ahead of time, please please tailor the email to the professor!! Contacting them earlier will help you understand whether they will be accepting students next year and whether you can work together, but a lot of schools have a centralized admissions process. This means that the professor will probably have no say in whether you are admitted or not, so do not take their response as a guaranteed admit. Remember, the professors are also extremely busy and may also not respond. However, I would recommend contacting professors for advice! A lot of them will be happy to share their thoughts on the application process and any concerns you may have.
